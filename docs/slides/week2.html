Deontology in Brief

SEP, Deontological Ethics

  • Focused on duties, rights, moral obligations (i.e. laws)
  • Laws might be prohibitions or obligations
  • Laws might come from different places
  • What is “right” vs what is “good”
  • Key figure: Immanuel Kant (1724 — 1804)

Kant (unknown artist, c. 1790), public domain

The Categorical Imperative

Act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law.

— Immanuel Kant

The Categorical Imperative

Act as if the maxims of your action were to become through your will a universal law of nature.

— Immanuel Kant

Categorical Imperative Examples

SEP, Deontological Ethics

  • Should I park at the airport terminal to wait?
    • If everyone did this…
    • More traffic issues?
  • Should I tell a lie?
    • If it were universally acceptable to lie…
    • How would we believe anyone?

Arrivals pickup and departures drop-off at O’Hare. Photo: AlphaBeta135, CC BY 4.0

The Right over the Good

SEP, Deontological Ethics

Deontology (from Greek deon, “duty”)

  • Some actions are required, forbidden, or permitted
  • Deontologists prioritize “the Right” over “the Good”
  • Some acts stay wrong even when those actions might bring about good consequences
  • (We will contrast with consequentialism, focused on outcomes)

“She Would Do Her Duty”

From Star Wars: Episode II — Attack of the Clones (2002), dir. George Lucas.

SEP, Deontological Ethics

The deontic square: every act is obligatory, permitted, omissible, or prohibited. Source: Wikipedia, “Deontic square”

Distinctions Within Deontology

Perspective

SEP, Deontological Ethics

Agent-centered

  • Focused on the “agent’s” own duties and intentions
    • Agent is a human :-)
  • “Keep your own moral house in order”
  • Agent-relative duties: e.g. obligations to your own children, promises, projects

Patient-centered

  • Focused on the “victim”
    • i.e. the recipient of an action
  • Rights-based (the duty to protect others’ rights)
  • Core right: to not be used as a means to an end

“Every Right, and Every Responsibility”

Screenplay excerpt from The Odyssey (2026).

Adolf Hirémy-Hirschl, Zwischen Skylla und Charybdis (c. 1910). Via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

Causing and Allowing

SEP, Deontological Ethics

  • Also called “doing vs. allowing harm”
  • Double effect: intending a harm differs from foreseeing it as a side effect
    • Categorically forbidden to intend evils like killing the innocent…
    • …even if doing so would reduce total killing!
  • Causing vs. omitting
  • Causing vs. allowing
  • Causing vs. enabling
  • Causing vs. redirecting
  • Causing vs. accelerating

Misc.

SEP, Deontological Ethics

  • Constraints: absolute prohibitions (even when breaking it could prevent more violations)
  • Threshold deontology: rules hold up to a point, so if the consequences are dire enough, forget about the rules
    • Difficult part: what is the exact threshold?
  • Alleged paradox of deontology: if one violation is wrong, aren’t more violations worse?

Alleged Paradox of Deontology

If respecting Mary’s and Susan’s rights is as important morally as is protecting John’s rights, then why isn’t violating John’s rights permissible (or even obligatory) when doing so is necessary to protect Mary’s and Susan’s rights from being violated?

— SEP, Deontological Ethics

Criticisms of Deontology

SEP, Deontological Ethics

  • Too permissive? Allows any outcome as long as no rule is broken
  • Conflicting duties: what if two duties collide?
  • The catastrophe problem: will we follow the rule even into disaster?
  • The murderer at the door: Benjamin Constant: must you tell the truth to a killer asking where your friend is hiding?

Trolley Problem Debrief

The Two Cases

Switch

Pull the lever to redirect the trolley onto a side track, killing one instead of five.

Footbridge

Push the man to stop the trolley, saving the five others.

Drawings by Jesse Prinz; wording after Chad Vance.

What Most People Say

90% of people say that you SHOULD kill the one in Switch (by pulling the lever), while 90–95% of people say that you SHOULD NOT kill the one in Footbridge — though the results are the same. In each case, the option is to kill 1, or let 5 die. What explains our difference of intuition in these two cases?

— Chad Vance, The Trolley Problem

Utilitarianism

SEP, The History of Utilitarianism

  • Based on the outcomes or consequences of an action
  • John Stuart Mill, Jeremy Bentham
  • Impartiality: “we all count the same in terms of our status as human beings”
  • Key questions
    • Whose well-being?
    • What is being used to define good/happiness/utility?
    • When is the measure of success being taken?

Bentham by Henry William Pickersgill, exhibited 1829, public domain

Two Sovereign Masters

Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do…

— Jeremy Bentham, An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), ch. I

Bentham’s Background

  • Bentham was a social reformer first, working on prisons, punishment, law, animal welfare
  • The Panopticon (1791): a prison laid out so that one watcher could observe everyone
    • No one could tell exactly when they were watched
  • Efficiency of surveillance as a utility argument

Plan of the Panopticon, from The Works of Jeremy Bentham vol. IV (1843). Via Wikimedia Commons, public domain

The Felicific Calculus

Sum up all the values of all the pleasures on the one side, and those of all the pains on the other. The balance, if it be on the side of pleasure, will give the good tendency of the act upon the whole.

— Jeremy Bentham, Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), ch. IV

Bentham’s Parameters of Pleasure

Bentham, Principles of Morals and Legislation, ch. IV, via SEP, The History of Utilitarianism

  • intensity — how strong
  • duration — how long it lasts
  • certainty or uncertainty — how likely
  • propinquity or remoteness — how soon
  • fecundity — chance of leading to more of the same
  • purity — chance of not leading to the opposite
  • extent — how many people

Bentham’s “Auto-Icon” — his preserved body, per his will — at UCL. Photo: Philip Stevens, CC BY-SA 4.0

Qualitative / quantitative differences in pleasures

Higher and Lower Pleasures

It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.

— John Stuart Mill, Utilitarianism (1863), ch. II

Mill’s Amendment

SEP, The History of Utilitarianism

  • Bentham: pleasures differ only in quantity
    • e.g. an oyster living a very long time could, in principle, beat a human life
    • Critics called this a “swine morality”
  • Mill: pleasures also differ in kind
    • can judge if you have experienced both

Mill, 19th-century photogravure, public domain

Happiness

Actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness.

— John Stuart Mill, Utilitarianism (1863), ch. II

Happiness

By happiness is intended pleasure, and the absence of pain; by unhappiness, pain, and the privation of pleasure.

— John Stuart Mill, Utilitarianism (1863), ch. II

Absolute Impartiality

  • If everybody counts for one, distance does not discount anyone
  • Peter Singer (1972): a drowning child in front of you and a preventable death overseas are the same moral fact
  • In this way, utilitarianism can be demanding

Utilitarian Approach to the Prisoner’s Dilemma

  • Testifying is better for you, regardless of what the other person does
  • Both of you reasoning that way lands on 8 years each, when 1 each was available
  • Robert Axelrod (1984): play it repeatedly and cooperation can win — “tit for tat” was the shortest program entered in his tournament

The Transplant Case

doethics.fun/dilemmas/utilitarian-ethics-transplant

A surgeon has five patients, each needing a different organ, each of whom will die without it. A healthy young traveler comes in for a routine checkup, and turns out to be compatible with all five. If the traveler were to disappear, no one would suspect the surgeon.

The arithmetic is the same as Footbridge: one dies, five live. Almost nobody accepts it.

The Transplant Case in Real Life

Dr. Hootan C. Roozrokh, a transplant surgeon from Stanford, is being charged with ordering the removal of a life-supporting ventilator and prescribing excessive and improper doses of drugs, apparently in an attempt to hasten the death of a disabled and brain damaged man named Ruben Navarro in order to retrieve his organs sooner.

— S. Matthew Liao, The Transplant Case in Real Life (2008)

What the Real Case Adds

S. Matthew Liao, The Transplant Case in Real Life

  • Navarro was not healthy and was, by most accounts, near death — an easier case than Foot’s, for a consequentialist
  • His mother had consented to donation, but not to hastening his death
  • Her words afterward: “He didn’t deserve to be like that, to go that way… He died without dignity and sympathy and without respect.”
  • The organs were never successfully retrieved. Would success have made it more permissible?
