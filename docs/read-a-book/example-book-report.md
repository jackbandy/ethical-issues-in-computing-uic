Sample Book Report  

There are not too many books I can genuinely recommend on the topics of antitrust law, interoperability, and monopolization in the U.S. political economy. Shoshana Zuboff's *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* is thorough and detailed for those with an enormous appetite (or perseverance, as it took me about a year to finish the audiobook), and definitely not a "general audience" book. Doctorow's other books (e.g. *Chokepoint Capitalism*, *The Internet Con*) are fairly approachable, however, they were missing the name.  

The power of a name, i.e. a title, is impossible to understate. In *The Founder (2018)*, Ray Kroc is asked why he pursued the purchase of McDonald's when he could have just copied the system. In response, he [explains](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt4276820/quotes/?item=qt3321350) "It's not just the system, Dick. It's the name. That glorious name, McDonald's." Ruha Benjamin begins *Race After Technology* by discussing what's in a name: names encode information related to our identities, our families, and our histories. "Our names can open and shut doors," she says.  

The name "Enshittification" appears to have opened up many doors for Cory Doctorow. Although he has been beating on a similar drumkit for most of his career, it is the christening of this drumbeat – "enshittification" – that seems to have brought him the most attention. It may be to his "bittersweet satisfaction," but we are all the better for it.  

What exactly is enshittification? A number of dictionaries have tried and failed to provide a concise definition, including those who named it their word of the year ([American Dialect Society](https://americandialect.org/2023-word-of-the-year-is-enshittification/), [Macquarie Dictionary](https://www.macquariedictionary.com.au/word-of-the-year-2024/)). Based on Doctorow's explanations in the book, I am not convinced we can define enshittification in a single dictionary entry. Still, I will try to lay out the key ideas. Enshittification is when some technology product or service gets worse, and it gets worse in very systematic ways. Doctorow proposes a three-step process: (1) be good to a mass user base, (2) be good to business customers, (3) mediate between the users and business customers to extract as much value as possible. The fourth and final step, if you must, is that the product or service "becomes a giant pile of shit."  

The book describes several case studies of enshittification, with details about the symptoms, mechanisms, and epidemiology of the phenomena. According to Doctorow, products and services like Facebook, Amazon, iPhone, and Twitter have all been part of the "Great Enshittening" – the swift spread of business tactics and decision-making strategies that take fun, useful products like Instagram and make them completely miserable. The book's description of the process was intuitive and familiar, from big tech companies buying out all their potential competitors, to wage theft and exploitative pricing, to other kinds of "twiddling" and experimenting, to outright rent-seeking. (Distinct from mere profit-seeking, which aims to make a product/service worthy of purchase, rent-seeking aims to turn a product/service into a constant source of revenue by any means possible. For example, it's not enough to just buy a SNOO for \$1,695 you also have to [pay \$20 a month](https://www.consumerreports.org/babies-kids/bassinets/should-you-buy-a-snoo-smart-bassinet-for-your-newborn-a9587255697/).)  

The best part of this book, in my opinion, is the proposed cure. Many people seem to have a pretty strong sense that big tech's products and services got worse, and something needs to change. But what do we actually do about it? It was encouraging to read Doctorow's explanations of "The Cure," which he approaches from an epidemiological perspective. The epidemiological approach recognizes the social forces that spread enshittification, and the potential for prevention.  

Similar to approaching a real-world pandemic response, some actions must be systemic. For Doctorow, systemic actions include antitrust law (i.e. regulators doing their job and ensuring competition for digital products and services) and "administrable" regulation that prevents monopolies. He also recognizes a growing coalition around the cause of privacy. Crucially, the laws themselves are not enough. We also need everyday people who *know* the laws and work to make sure they are enforced – people who care enough about their rights to follow through. Similarly, "the cure" will require workers who are paying attention, standing up, and organizing to protect their pay and their working conditions.

For those of us who wish to work toward a cure, our rallying cry might be the [words of William Gibson](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2012/01/24/future-has-arrived/): "The future is already here, it's just not evenly distributed." By naming this uneven distribution "enshittification," Doctorow has helped us move toward curing it.
