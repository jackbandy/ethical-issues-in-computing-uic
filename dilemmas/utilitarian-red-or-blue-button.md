# Red Button or Blue Button

Everyone on earth takes a private vote by pressing a red or a blue button:

* If **more than 50% of people press blue**, everyone survives.
* If **less than 50% of people press blue**, only the people who pressed **red** survive.

Nobody learns what anyone else pressed until everyone has decided.

Here is how the dilemma looks as a "payoff table:"

|                     | **Over 50% press blue** | **Under 50% press blue**            |
| ------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| **You press blue**  | You survive             | You die                             |
| **You press red**   | You survive             | You survive                         |


As with other "matrix games," you only control which row you land in, while other people control which column you land in. Notably, pressing red means you survive no matter what anyone else does. 

_Which button would you press?_

Source: [Steven Conway, "Red button or blue button? What a viral question tells us about game theory and the state of the world," *The Conversation*, May 4, 2026](https://theconversation.com/red-button-or-blue-button-what-a-viral-question-tells-us-about-game-theory-and-the-state-of-the-world-281993)

Context: Conway frames the split as two different ideas of a "good" choice: "In game theory, this choice [red] leads to what is known as the Nash equilibrium. This is the best choice for a participant looking to advance their own interests." Blue, by contrast, "hint[s] at what game theorists call the Pareto-optimal outcome, where the least potential damage is done by one's choice."

Context: Conway also discusses *reasons* given by people who pressed blue: "I'm worried my family and friends might pick blue and I want them to survive"; "I'm concerned people might find out if I pick red and judge me"; "If I picked red I would feel responsible for the potential deaths of others"; "I believe humanity is inherently good".

Context: On why the question went viral in 2026, Conway invokes Günther Anders' 1956 "Promethean gap" which says "the more technological capacity grows, the less humanity can comprehend emotionally, intellectually and morally." He concludes, "our capacity for action exceeds our capacities for moral imagination." He notes the stakes make older puzzles (e.g. the trolley problem) "seem positively quaint."
